Five traps in your own spreadsheets

None of this is about which tools to use. It is about your data — and it stays true whether you build on Supabase, a database, or a plain JSON file.

Found while reading the League History, Power Rankings and Excel workbook you shared. Two of these produce answers that look completely correct and are not.

The traps

Tagged by whether the bug tells you it happened.

The loud ones are fine — you will hit them and fix them in a minute. The silent ones are the reason this document exists: they produce complete, plausible, confidently wrong output, and nothing anywhere reports a problem.

Silent · gets every champion wrong

1. Excel Drop has three columns called Name

That tab lays several unrelated blocks side by side and repeats header names between them. Name appears at column 4, column 11 and column 41.

Most spreadsheet readers key rows by header name, and the last match quietly wins. So the finishes block's placings get paired with a different block's list of managers.

What that looked like A full championship list, one champion per year, entirely believable — and wrong in all ten years. It named a 4–10 team as a champion. Nothing crashed, nothing rendered empty, no error appeared. It was caught only by checking the output against the Banners tab.

The fix: tell the reader which span of columns the block owns — here, columns 8 through 27 — and resolve names only inside it. Then check the result against Banners, every time.

Silent · doubles every count

2. GameData stores every game twice

There is an A/B column. Each matchup appears once from each team's point of view — an A row and a B row.

Counting rows gives you double the games. Summing points counts every point twice. Win totals come out right, which is what makes this one sneaky: half your numbers look fine.

The shape of it 1,804 rows for 902 real games. Build the game list from the A rows only; build per-team stats from all of them.

Worth asserting that the two sides are balanced, and that no row has an A/B value that is neither A nor B. If that ever stops being true, something changed upstream and you want to know.

Silent · merges two people's careers

3. Twenty managers, and the short names collide

No amount of clever code resolves this from the sheets alone. Across RBB and Dyno Mites there are twenty people, and the names your sheets use are ambiguous:

  • Two Perkinses — Jimmie and Jim
  • Three Joneses — Josh, Austin, Nick
  • Two Malaks — Joe and Jerry
  • Two Amezcuas — Mike and Arturo
  • Two Joshes, two Joes, two Jonathans

Then there are nicknames that share nothing with the real name. Yisha is Josh Baker — confirmed three independent ways, including that Banners credits Josh Baker with 2018 and 2022, exactly where Finishes says Yisha. And Jacube is Jacob Schmiegelt.

Why it is worth the effort Guess wrong and two people's entire careers merge into one, or one person's splits in two. Every record, every head-to-head and every title count is then wrong, and it will look completely normal.

The fix: a hand-written list mapping every spelling to one person, and code that stops when it meets a name that is not on the list rather than guessing. Fifteen of the twenty are in RBB; Gil Smit is active in RBB but retired from Dyno Mites, so membership is per-league, not global.

Silent · reads as a score

4. Best BN over STRT is a yes/no, not an amount

It looks like a margin. It is a 0 or 1 flag meaning "the best bench player outscored a starter." The actual margin is in BN Gap.

Sort a bench-regret leaderboard by the wrong one and you get a list ordered by a column that only ever holds 0 or 1 — which produces a ranking that renders perfectly and means nothing.

One more from the same area When you do sort by BN Gap, two rows tie at exactly 37.95. Without an explicit tiebreak the "worst bench decision ever" changes between runs. Break ties on year, then week, then manager.
Loud · you will notice this one

5. Formula scaffolding and text in number columns

Two small things that will stop a naive import, which is the good outcome:

  • Rows at the bottom of some tabs are formula scaffolding rather than data — they carry __ where an ID should be. Skip any row missing its key column, and count what you skipped so a real problem cannot hide there.
  • Number columns sometimes hold words: Undrafted, Bye, and an en-dash for "nothing here". Treat those as empty rather than letting them become zero — a zero is a real value and will drag averages down.

Reading your Google Sheets automatically

No API key, no service account, no OAuth.

Google will hand over an entire spreadsheet as an .xlsx file from a plain URL, as long as the sheet is shared as "anyone with the link can view": 

https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/<id>/export?format=xlsx

This is a much better door than the per-tab CSV endpoint, for three reasons:

  • One request for the whole document, not one per tab.
  • It includes hidden tabs — which matters, because you hide your formula machinery, and CSV cannot even enumerate those.
  • The file goes through the same reader as a local .xlsx, so what you test against is what you get.

It is proven, not theoretical

A scheduled job pulled both sheets unattended this morning — 4,049 KB of League History and 518 KB of Power Rankings — with no credentials involved. That is the whole "site updates itself" problem solved, and it costs nothing.

The one catch: if link-sharing is ever revoked, Google returns a sign-in page with a success status rather than an error. Check that what came back actually starts with PK — an .xlsx file is a zip — or you will cheerfully parse an HTML login page.

Also worth knowing

Your Power Rankings sheet has an All Weeks tab holding every weekly ranking and write-up you have published since 2019 — 1,392 rows across seven seasons. It is the single richest thing in any of the five sheets, and it is already structured. Read it rather than recomputing it: they are your rankings, with your reasoning attached, and a formula that disagreed with the number you published would just be wrong.

One open question I could not answer from the files: where does 2025 lineup data live? Neither Google sheet has lineup rows at all, and the Excel workbook stops at 2024. So anything bench-related — best-player-you-benched, roster detail — currently ends at 2024.

No pressure either way; the traps above are the part that is genuinely hard to rediscover.
